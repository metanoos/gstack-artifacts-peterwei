# Peter’s Builder Journey

You began by trying to make AI-native writing auditions singular enough to be legible: one concrete way for a writer to show how a piece came into being. The early work separated provenance that merely sounds important from evidence that actually changes a writer’s, reader’s, or evaluator’s decision.

Citation rendezvous widened the project from an individual writing record into a social object. That expansion exposed the temptation to turn every useful relationship into a protocol feature: live stages, shared editing, profiles, queries, witnesses, capabilities, and feed topology. You repeatedly noticed when the concepts stopped helping the experience. “We’re turning collaboration tools into performance tools” was not rejection; it was a demand that the pivot be named before it became architecture.

The asynchronous Zine press kept the strongest idea—the production and consumption trace can be part of the work—while removing the assumption that its value requires live transport. Public interaction became Reply and Replay; permissioned construction became asynchronous; voices stopped masquerading as signers; Step and Send became separate acts.

Then the personal instrument became the center. Instead of asking whether a reader would admire the trace, you asked whether you would trust Zine as the place where essays, notes, sources, and model conversations actually live. **Writing Under Observation** became the first authentic work rather than a demonstration assembled after the fact.

Session eight makes the next correction explicit. You said the history should include “not just deletions but late insertions; and selection state” and asked for “a full replay of the text composition process.” When a deletion-first overlay could not honestly align historical geometry to final text, you chose two linked truths: a Composition Map over the present manuscript and Exact Replay of historical states.

You widened file replay again when the lived workflow demanded it: “a folder replay is the composition of all actions taken inside of it.” That forced replay to span files, columns, and tab lists without duplicating events into ancestor traces. Then you subtracted just as decisively. “No need for legacy support” removed Step instead of preserving a hidden compatibility fossil. When the wireframe gave every file its own playbar row, you immediately saw that a real folder would make it too tall; the permanent lanes collapsed into a fixed transport and an activity drawer.

By session ten, you turned the same scrutiny outward. The question stopped being how complete the trace could become and became whether anyone’s evaluation actually changes because of it. You accepted that the project has no direct demand evidence, treated the existing assessment protocol—not Google Docs—as the competitor, and put product work behind a discovery gate. When Contentellect became a convenient prospect, you removed it because you “do not trust the company/brand,” then replaced it with a deliberate sequence: Ashley Strosnider for buyer discovery, Karl Hughes for operator reality, and Gordana Sretenovic for competitive truth.

Across ten office-hours sessions and seven design documents, the recurring pattern is not that your ideas simply get larger or smaller. They get more load-bearing. You expand the object until it matches the real activity, then remove every concept—or comfortable assumption—that cannot justify its own existence. Your taste is increasingly architectural and commercial: exact text but not false pixels, settled selection but not surveillance, folder-wide replay but not duplicated authority, process evidence but not covert detection, and a real buyer commitment before more product scope.

Do not demo Zine during the first 20 minutes. The work now is to discover whether the uncertainty exists in her words before asking the product to solve it.
